Rent Freeze

Examining the New York City 2026 rent freeze, and it's effects for its stakeholders.

Background

In New York City, rising rent and living expenses have made housing affordability a growing concern for many residents. To address this issue, the city uses rent stabilization, a system that limits how much landlords can increase rent for certain apartments. The New York City Rent Guidelines Board (RGB) plays an important role in this process by determining the maximum annual rent increases for rent-stabilized apartments.
In 2026, the Rent Guidelines Board voted 7-1 to freeze rents for both one-year and two-year leases beginning between October 1, 2026, and September 30, 2027. This decision means that tenants with qualifying leases would not experience a rent increase during that period. While the freeze aims to provide financial relief for tenants struggling with housing costs, it has also raised concerns among landlords about their ability to cover rising operating and maintenance expenses.
The rent freeze has sparked an ongoing debate about balancing affordable housing with the financial responsibilities of property owners. Through our interactive website, we explore the history of rent regulation, examine the experiences of tenants and landlords, and use data to better understand the potential benefits and challenges of the 2026 rent freeze.

Is my lease frozen?

  • Yes, if it's a rent-stabilized lease that starts between Oct. 1, 2026 and Sept. 30, 2027. The start date counts, not the day you signed.
  • A two-year renewal that starts in that window is frozen for both years.
  • Leases that started before Oct. 1, 2026 follow last year's rates: 3% for one year, 4.5% for two.
  • Market-rate apartments are not covered.

Background

1969Fact

The Rent Guidelines Board is created

A nine-member board, appointed by the mayor, starts setting the maximum yearly rent increase for stabilized apartments.

Source: Rent Guidelines Board

2015 and 2016Fact

First freezes, on one-year leases only

Under Mayor de Blasio, the board set 0% for one-year leases two years in a row. Two-year leases still rose 2%.

Source: RGB rent order history (PDF)

March 2017Legal challenge

A judge upholds the freezes

Landlords argued the freezes were political. A state judge ruled the board is allowed to consider whether tenants can afford increases.

Source: New York Daily News

2019

June 14, 2019Fact

New state law limits how rents can rise

The Housing Stability and Tenant Protection Act ended most ways landlords could raise stabilized rents beyond the board's yearly vote, such as the 20% bonus when a tenant moved out. After this, the yearly vote mattered much more.

Source: NYU Furman Center

June 25, 2019Fact

Board votes 1.5% and 2.5%

One-year leases rose 1.5% and two-year leases 2.5%. A proposal to freeze rents failed.

Source: Gothamist

2020–2021

March 2020Fact

COVID halts evictions

The state paused evictions at the start of the pandemic. The protections lasted until January 2022.

Source: NYC Bar Association

June 17, 2020Fact

Pandemic freeze on one-year leases

The board froze one-year leases. Two-year leases got 0% the first year and 1% the second, so this was not a full two-year freeze.

Source: NY1

June 23, 2021Fact

Half a year at 0%, then 1.5%

One-year leases were frozen for six months, then rose 1.5%. Two-year leases rose 2.5%.

Source: The Real Deal

2022–2024

2022 to 2024Fact

Three years of increases under Mayor Adams

The board raised one-year leases 3.25% (2022), 3% (2023) and 2.75% (2024). Two-year increases ran from about 4.5% to 5.25%. The 2022 vote was the largest increase in about a decade.

Source: Rent Guidelines Board

October 23, 2024Political promise

Mamdani runs for mayor on "Freeze the Rent"

Assemblymember Zohran Mamdani launched his campaign. A rent freeze for stabilized tenants became his signature promise.

Source: NY1

2025

June 24, 2025Fact

Mamdani wins the Democratic primary

He beat former Governor Andrew Cuomo with about 56% in the final ranked-choice count. A freeze went from a campaign slogan to a likely policy.

Source: PBS NewsHour

June 30, 2025Fact

Last increase before the freeze: 3% and 4.5%

Six days after the primary, the board raised one-year leases 3% and two-year leases 4.5%. These rates still apply to leases that started before Oct. 1, 2026.

Source: Rent Guidelines Board

November 4, 2025Fact

Mamdani elected mayor

He won with about 51% of the vote.

Source: Ballotpedia

December 18, 2025Fact

Outgoing Mayor Adams fills board seats

With two weeks left in office, Adams named four board members, seen as an attempt to block a freeze. One appointee backed out days later, opening a seat for Mamdani.

Source: NY1

2026

February 18, 2026Fact

Mamdani appoints six of nine board members

After several Adams-era members resigned, Mamdani named six members, including chair Chantella Mitchell, giving him a majority.

Source: Mayor's office

March–April 2026Fact

The board's yearly research reports

Landlord net income rose 6.2% citywide but fell in the Bronx. Building costs rose 5.3%. Over half of NYC renters spend 30% or more of income on rent. The income figures leave out mortgage payments, which is why both sides cite them.

Source: RGB Income and Expense Study (PDF)

April 16, 2026Political promise

City promises cheaper insurance for landlords

Mamdani announced a city-backed insurance program meant to cut premiums 20–30%, starting in 2027. The savings are projections.

Source: The Real Deal

May 7, 2026Fact

Preliminary vote puts 0% on the table

The board proposed a range of 0–2% for one-year leases and 0–4% for two-year leases.

Source: The City

May 2026Fact

City budget office checks building conditions

Most stabilized buildings are not in worse shape than other rentals. Serious problems are concentrated in a small share of buildings, mostly in the Bronx and Upper Manhattan.

Source: NYC Independent Budget Office (PDF)

June 25, 2026Fact

The board votes 7–1 to freeze rents

0% on both one-year and two-year leases starting Oct. 1, 2026 — the first two-year freeze ever. Economist Arpit Gupta cast the only no vote. A landlord member resigned that morning, saying the result was decided in advance.

Source: NY1

July 22, 2026Legal challenge

Landlords sue to overturn the freeze

A group of landlords sued in Staten Island, calling the vote a "sham process with a predetermined outcome." The city says the board acted on its own.

Note: one report gives the filing date as July 21.

Source: Gothamist

August 21, 2026Legal challenge

Case moves to Manhattan

A judge ruled the lawsuit belonged in Manhattan. This did not decide whether the freeze is legal.

Source: The Real Deal

September 2026Legal challenge

Judge orders City Hall to hand over messages

Justice Brendan Lantry ordered the mayor's office and board members to turn over their emails and texts. He refused to pause the freeze while the case continues.

Source: Gothamist

October 1, 2026Fact

The freeze takes effect

Renewing tenants now get offers at the same rent as last year. It is not yet clear what happens to these leases if the court overturns the vote.

Source: NY1

What's next

By end of 2026Upcoming

Judge expected to rule

Lantry said he would decide by year's end whether the vote was lawful. Either side is expected to appeal if it loses.

Source: amNY

Spring–summer 2027Upcoming

The next yearly vote

The board will vote again on leases starting Oct. 1, 2027. Mamdani has said he wants a freeze every year, but a second freeze is a promise, not a decision.

Source: Rent Guidelines Board

Tenants

The graphs below highlight the experiences and challenges faced by tenants in New York City. We look at the differences between market-rate and rent-stabilized tenants specifically their age, economic status, living conditions, etc.

Where the Freeze Lands

The freeze covers about 1 million apartments, but they are not spread evenly. In the Bronx, two out of every three private renters live in a rent-stabilized apartment. Darker red means more of a borough's renters are covered. Hover over a borough to see the median income of its rent-stabilized households.

The Freeze Hits the Bronx Hardest

The people in rent-stabilized apartments are not like other New York renters. Compared with market-rate renters, they are older, lower-income and about twice as likely to have fallen behind on rent. Each row compares the two groups with the gray dot being the market-rate renters, the red dot is rent-stabilized renters, and the line between them being the gap.

Rent-Stabilized Renters are Older, Lower-Income, and More Likely to Fall Behind on Rent

The typical rent-stabilized household earns $60,000 a year, about two-thirds of what market-rate renters earn. When you compare the red and gray bars in each income group we find that rent-stabilized households cluster at the low end, and market-rate households at the high end. For a tenant paying the median stabilized rent of $1,500, skipping a 3% increase in your rent saves about $540 a year.

1 in 4 Rent-Stabilized Households Earn Under $25,000

The map above shows the pattern while this chart gives the exact numbers. The Bronx stands apart, with 67.2% of its private renters in rent-stabilized apartments. Brooklyn and Manhattan are nearly tied at about 44% and Queens is at 38%.

Two-Thirds of Bronx Private Renters are Rent-Stabilized

Not every tenant feels the freeze the same way. About one in five rent-stabilized households already gets help paying rent. For tenants with a Section 8 voucher (red), rent is tied to income, so the freeze mostly lowers what the government pays, not what the tenant pays. The gray section shows other aid, such as programs that already freeze rent for seniors and people with disabilities.

About 1 in 5 Rent-Stabilized Households Already get Help Paying Rent

Landlords

The graphs below highlight the experiences and challenges faced by landlords in New York City. We look at the differences between their operating costs and stabilized rent income revenue specifically how stabilized units affect landlords income status.

Landlords say the math no longer works. For nine years in a row, the cost of running a building has grown faster than the rents they collect. Watch the gap between the dark line (costs) and the red line (rents). Now look at 2026, where costs rose 5.3% and the freeze set rent increases at 0%.

Since 2017, Operating Costs Have Risen Faster Than Stabilized Rents Every Year

Landlord income after operating costs still rose 6.2% in 2024, but that average hides who is struggling. The more rent-stabilized apartments a building has, the less its income grew. Each bar down the chart is a building type with more stabilized units, and each one gained less.

The More Stabilized the Building, the Smaller the Gain

Conclusion

The New York City 2026 rent freeze shows the challenges that can arise from balancing housing affordability and the financial costs of maintaining housing conditions. Our research has found that rent-stabilized tenants tend to have lower income and experience more housing problems than market-rate tenants. For these tenants, the freeze is a welcome relief. However, landlords have expressed concerns about the financial impact of the freeze on their ability to maintain and improve their properties. The legal challenge to the freeze highlights the ongoing debate over the role of government in regulating housing markets. As the situation continues to unfold, it will be important to monitor the effects of the freeze on both tenants and landlords, as well as the broader implications for housing policy in New York City.

Glossary

Rent Guidelines Board
The nine-member city board that sets annual rent increase amounts for rent-stabilized apartments. All nine members are appointed by the mayor. Created in 1969.
Rent Freeze
A 0% increase set by the RGB for a given lease cycle. Not a law, it's a yearly vote, so a "freeze" only lasts one cycle unless the board votes for it again the next year.
Rent Stabilization
A NYC system that limits how much landlords can raise rent each year on covered apartments, and gives tenants the right to renew their lease. About 1 million apartments in NYC are rent-stabilized, roughly 40-43% of the city's rental housing stock.
Rent Control
A separate, much smaller and older system than rent stabilization. Applies only to tenants who have lived continuously in an apartment since 1971 (or their descendants). Not the focus of this project, but easy to confuse with rent stabilization.
HSTPA
The Housing Stability and Tenant Protection Act. A 2019 New York state law that closed most of the ways landlords could previously raise rents on stabilized apartments (like charging more after a tenant moved out). Widely cited as the reason landlord costs and rents have diverged more sharply since 2019.
NOI
Net Operating Income. A building's income (mostly rent) minus its operating costs (not counting mortgage payments). This is the number RGB reports use to describe whether landlords are "doing fine" or "struggling", but it does NOT include debt payments, which is a common point of confusion in the debate.
Operating Costs
The cost of running a building: fuel, insurance, repairs, property taxes, water/sewer, staff. Does not include mortgage or debt payments.
PIOC
Price Index of Operating Costs. The RGB's own yearly measurement of how much landlord operating costs are rising. This is the source of the "landlord costs rose X%" stat you'll see cited everywhere.
I&E Study
Income and Expense Study. The RGB's annual report comparing landlord income to landlord costs, building by building, using tax filings. This is the most detailed, most-cited data source in the whole debate. Both landlords and tenant advocates use numbers from this report.
Distressed Building
An RGB term for a building whose rental income doesn't cover its operating costs. Currently about 9.2% of rent-stabilized buildings, according to the RGB's own numbers — and that share has been falling, not rising.
Rent Burden
"Rent burdened" = spending 30%+ of income on rent. "Severely rent burdened" = spending 50%+. Over half of NYC renter households are rent burdened; almost 30% are severely rent burdened.
Warehousing
When a landlord deliberately keeps an apartment empty instead of renting it out — usually because renovating it isn't worth the cost under current rent rules.
